Simulating the Global Rise of
Antimicrobial Resistance

An individual-based model linking antibiotic use, bacterial biology, and health systems

42 Bacterial species
61 Antibiotics
40 Resistance mechanisms
6 World regions
105Years (1930 – 2035)

Why?

Antimicrobial resistance — bacteria surviving antibiotic treatment — is one of the most serious threats to global health. Understanding how resistance emerges, spreads, and responds to policy changes requires a model that captures the interplay between drug use, microbial evolution, and healthcare delivery.

What?

A stochastic, individual-based simulation that creates ~100,000 virtual people and processes each one through 21 mechanistic rules every day for over a century. Starting in 1930 — before antibiotics existed — the model reproduces the entire historical arc of drug introduction, rising consumption, and the gradual accumulation of resistance.

Scope

  • 42 bacteria — from E. coli and S. aureus to M. tuberculosis and N. gonorrhoeae
  • 61 antibiotics in 39 drug classes — sulfonamides (1937) through cefiderocol (2021)
  • 40 resistance mechanisms — ESBLs, carbapenemases (KPC, NDM, OXA-48), efflux pumps, target modifications, etc.
  • 6 world regions with distinct drug access, hospital capacity, testing infrastructure, travel, and pathogen mix
  • Correlated resistance profiles — new infections inherit multi-mechanism genotypes from a circulating-strain reservoir

How the Model Works

Each person is processed through a clinical journey every simulated day

Demographics
age · sex · region · immunity → Infection
Acquisition → Clinical
Progression → Diagnostic
Testing → Antibiotic
Treatment → Resistance
Dynamics → Mortality

Key Subsystems

  • Infection acquisition — community, hospital, or endogenous (carrier → infection). Logistic risk model with region, age, season, and era terms
  • Clinical progression — 10 syndromes (UTI, respiratory, bloodstream …), continuous bacterial level, sepsis
  • Diagnostic testing — bacterial culture + AST; 3-day turnaround; regional lab capacity from ×0.3 (Africa) to ×1.2 (Europe)
  • Antibiotic prescribing — empiric (before results) → targeted (after AST); drug scoring, spectrum, toxicity, potency
  • Resistance emergence — de novo mutation + horizontal gene transfer (HGT) between co-colonising species; mechanism-level tracking with reversion and fitness costs
  • Microbiome carriage — asymptomatic reservoir; resistant genes survive antibiotic courses, seed future infections, and transfer via HGT

Calibration Anchors

Parameters are tuned so that model outputs match real-world surveillance data:

  • Antibiotic consumption rates (DDD/1000/day)
  • Resistance prevalence for each bacteria × drug combination from ECDC, CDC, and WHO GLASS
  • Infection incidence and case-fatality rates

Implementation

  • Written in Rust for speed — a single 105-year run on 100,000 (1 million) people completes in 70 minutes (8 hours)
  • ~11,000 parameters auto-generated from structured configuration
  • Stochastic — repeated runs yield a distribution of outcomes, not a single trajectory
  • Compact 64-bit bitmask per person for all 40 resistance mechanisms

Evaluating Counterfactuals and Alternative Policies

One shared history branches into parallel futures at 2027 — differences are causally attributable

Baseline

Business as usual. No policy changes — the current prescribing environment continues unchanged into the future.

Examples of Alternative Policies

Antibiotic stewardship interventions: narrower prescribing, more testing, shorter courses, stronger reserve-drug barriers.

Counterfactual

Resistance is eliminated at the branch point — quantifies the total burden attributable to AMR by comparison.

Measurable Outcomes

  • Resistance prevalence trends over time, per bacterium × drug × region
  • Deaths from resistant vs. susceptible infections
  • Treatment failures and time to effective therapy
  • Antibiotic consumption (DDD per 1000 per day)
  • Hospital-acquired vs. community-acquired AMR

Why Branching Works

All three futures start from an identical population snapshot — every person's age, infections, microbiome resistance, and treatment history is the same. Any difference in outcomes is therefore causally attributable, within the model, to the policy differences alone.
